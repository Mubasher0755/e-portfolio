# e-portfolio-5-Automated-Decision-Making
A collection of artefacts that demonstrate what I have learnt about the ethical and social issues of Automated Decision Making (ADM) in government systems.

Before I started this unit, I honestly thought ADM was a good thing for government. I believed a computer would be faster and fairer than a person because it follows the same rules for everyone. During this term my view changed a lot. After learning about ethical theories, the ACS Code of Professional Conduct and real cases like Robodebt, I now see that ADM can hurt thousands of people when there is no transparency, no legal check and no real human oversight. My six artefacts follow the path of my learning: what ADM is, what went wrong in Australia and overseas, why it went wrong, and how it can be fixed.

## Artefact 1: What is Automated Decision Making (ADM)?

<img width="504" height="743" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/6f3650ac-d5a1-4690-af23-1b5a03374e24" />



https://www.ombudsman.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0025/317437/Automated-Decision-Making-Better-Practice-Guide-March-2025.pdf

### Summary of the artefact
This is a guide from the Commonwealth Ombudsman, updated in March 2025, for Australian government agencies that use automation. It describes an automated system as "a computer system that automates part or all of an administrative decision-making process" (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2025, p. 7). The guide says these systems must follow the principles of "legality, fairness, rationality and transparency" (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2025, p. 8). It also says people should be told when automation is used and should be able to challenge the decision (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2025).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I chose this guide as my starting point because it gave me a clear and official definition of ADM. Before, I saw ADM only as software. Now I understand it is a real government decision that affects people's lives. I found it important that an agency should name a person with "ultimate responsibility for the decision" (Commonwealth Ombudsman, 2025, p. 15). This links to the ACS value of Professionalism, because we cannot blame the computer when something goes wrong.

## Artefact 2: Robodebt – what happens when ADM goes wrong in Australia

<img width="1919" height="1079" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8ffe0e4a-14f5-4adc-9f27-ff6a9174103a" />


https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BKPQ1iZgAL8

### Summary of the artefact
After learning what ADM should look like, I wanted to see a real Australian example. This ABC News video from September 2025 reports that the federal government agreed to pay another $475 million to victims of Robodebt (ABC News (Australia), 2025). Robodebt was an automated scheme that wrongly told welfare recipients they owed money. The Attorney-General called the settlement "the just and fair thing to do", and lawyers said more than 450,000 Australians would benefit (Dick, 2025).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I chose this video because it shows the human cost of ADM, and that the damage continues many years later. When I applied Act Utilitarianism, I could see that the money the government wanted to save was much smaller than the harm and the cost of compensation. From a Kantian view, people were treated only as a means to get money back. It reminded me that the ACS Code puts the Primacy of the Public Interest first.

## Artefact 3: Comparing Robodebt with the Dutch scandal

<img width="1901" height="911" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/bdae8055-44a8-4982-82ff-11e519b24edd" />


https://doi.org/10.1177/13882627251330438

### Summary of the artefact
Next, I wanted to understand why Robodebt happened and whether other countries had the same problem. This journal article by Bouwmeester (2025) compares Robodebt with the Dutch childcare benefits scandal. He explains that the Robodebt algorithm "'averaged' earnings over the period of a full year" (Bouwmeester, 2025, p. 106). In the Netherlands, "at least 26,000 benefit recipients were wrongly treated as deliberate fraudsters" (Bouwmeester, 2025, p. 109). He argues that "Automation played only a minor part in this bigger play" (Bouwmeester, 2025, p. 112).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I chose this article because it changed my first opinion. At the start, I blamed only the algorithm. This article showed me that harsh policies and political pressure behind a system decide whether it is used fairly. It links to Social Contract Theory, because in both countries the government ignored the checks that protect citizens. As a future ICT professional, I learned to ask why a system is being built, not only how.

## Artefact 4: The Dutch childcare benefits scandal – ADM in another country

<img width="800" alt="RSM Discovery article Kafkaesque AI" src="PASTE-IMAGE-LINK-HERE">

https://www.rsm.nl/discovery/2025/shifting-the-burden-of-proof-back-to-ai/

### Summary of the artefact
The journal article made me want to look more closely at the Netherlands. This article from the Rotterdam School of Management (January 2025) is based on a roundtable with legal and academic experts. It explains how fraud-detection algorithms in the Dutch childcare benefits scandal flagged vulnerable citizens, and the systems were so unclear that people could barely challenge the decisions (Rotterdam School of Management, 2025). The experts recommend public AI registers, human review, bias testing and independent oversight.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I chose this artefact because it shows that ADM problems are global, not only Australian. The idea that AI shifts the burden of proof onto citizens surprised me, because the same thing happened with Robodebt. This links to the ACS value of Honesty, because a system that cannot explain its decisions is not being honest with people. It also made me understand why bias testing is part of a developer's ethical duty.

## Artefact 5: Is a "human in the loop" enough?
<img width="1900" height="911" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/23a1bc5b-43bc-472e-8999-ed9632128ad7" />


https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2025-09-23-techdispatch-22025-human-oversight-automated-making_en

### Summary of the artefact
Both cases made me think that adding a human reviewer is the simple answer. This TechDispatch from the European Data Protection Supervisor (September 2025) tests that idea. It explains that adding a human does not automatically make decisions better, because of automation bias, where people accept what the system says without questioning it (European Data Protection Supervisor, 2025). Real oversight needs staff with authority, training and time, and it cannot fix a badly designed system.

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I chose this artefact because it corrected my belief that a human reviewer solves every risk. Now I understand that if the human cannot really change the result, they are only a rubber stamp. This connects to Virtue Ethics, because good oversight needs people with the courage to disagree with a machine. It also links to the ACS value of Competence, since ICT professionals must understand the limits of the systems they build.

## Artefact 6: The future of ADM rules in Australia

<img width="800" alt="Medical Republic article on ADM Bill 2026" src="PASTE-IMAGE-LINK-HERE">

https://www.medicalrepublic.com.au/no-more-robodebt-new-push-for-guardrails-on-government-algorithms/128891

### Summary of the artefact
Finally, I wanted to know what Australia is doing now to stop another Robodebt. This news article from September 2026 reports that independent MP Kate Chaney introduced the Automated Decision-Making (Safeguards and Transparency) Bill 2026. The Bill aims to put Robodebt Royal Commission recommendations 17.1 and 17.2 into law, with transparency about where ADM is used, human oversight for high-risk decisions and a fast right to review (Wright, 2026).

### Justification on why I chose the artefact
I chose this artefact because it connects my learning to what is happening in Australia right now. I was surprised when Chaney said there are "still no guardrails around the use of automation across government" (Wright, 2026). Using Rule Utilitarianism, I think a rule that requires transparency and review would bring the best result for society. It made me realise ICT professionals should build these safeguards into every system.

## Conclusion
Looking back, my view of ADM has changed from trust to careful questioning. ADM can make government faster, but only when it is lawful, transparent, tested for bias and controlled by humans who can really say no. As a future ICT professional, I will use the ACS Code and ethical theories to make sure the systems I build serve the public, not only the budget.
