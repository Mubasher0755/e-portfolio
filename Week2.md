**Source:** [What Is AI? | Learn all about artificial intelligence](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JcXKbUlebrU&t=14s)

### Summary of the artefact[cite: 1]
I looked at a short YouTube video by Learn Bright that breaks down Artificial Intelligence into easy-to-understand terms. It outlines three main categories: Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI), Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), and Artificial Super Intelligence (ASI)[cite: 1]. The video also covers some of the major pros and cons of AI in modern society[cite: 1]. I used to think of AI simply as tech designed to act like humans, but after watching this, I realize it’s really about complex systems handling massive datasets to solve specific problems[cite: 1].

### Justification on why I chose the artefact[cite: 1]
I picked this video because it kept things simple and avoided overloading me with tech jargon[cite: 1]. While I’d heard of AGI before, ANI and ASI were totally new concepts to me[cite: 1]. The idea of ASI is a little intimidating—especially after our class discussion on how people might form real emotional connections with machines[cite: 1]. I was relieved when the narrator pointed out that ASI is still purely theoretical and pretty far-fetched for now[cite: 1]. Even though the video seems targeted at a younger audience, I find that straightforward visual explanations are the best way to grasp new topics[cite: 1]. As a first-year student, I'm still building up my foundational knowledge, so this was a great starting point[cite: 1]!

### Summary of the artefact[cite: 1]
This news article by Melissa Brown (2026) highlights how wildlife rangers in Victoria are using AI to safeguard native animal species[cite: 1]. Normally, rangers collect thousands of trail camera images that take weeks of manual labor to sort through[cite: 1]. Now, a new model trained on 5 million images of Australian fauna can process 20 frames per second with over 95% accuracy[cite: 1]. This saves enormous amounts of time and budget, allowing scientists to respond much faster to environmental threats like feral pigs[cite: 1]. Best of all, the developers made the tool open-source so local communities can use it too[cite: 1].

### Justification on why I chose the artefact[cite: 1]
With so much news focusing on the negative impacts of AI, I wanted to choose something genuinely inspiring[cite: 1]. This tied directly into our workshop discussion about machine learning's power to analyze large datasets rapidly[cite: 1]. Seeing advanced image recognition being used to protect endangered animals shows how technology can be a force for good[cite: 1]. The article itself was really engaging, with clean visuals and an interactive slider that demonstrated how the AI flags camouflaged animals a human viewer would easily miss[cite: 1]. I also appreciate that the creators made it open-source while taking data governance and intellectual property rights seriously[cite: 1].

## Artefact 3: Scholarly Article[cite: 1]

![Scholarly Article Header](URL_TO_YOUR_PAPER_HEADER_IMAGE_HERE)[cite: 1]

**Source:** [AI-driven healthcare: A review on ensuring fairness and mitigating bias](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000864)[cite: 1]

### Summary of the artefact[cite: 1]
This review paper by Chinta et al. (2025) examines how AI is transforming medical sectors like cardiology, dermatology, and emergency medicine[cite: 1]. Machine learning models are driving better diagnostic accuracy and personalized treatment plans, but the authors warn that algorithmic bias poses major ethical risks[cite: 1]. If the underlying data is flawed or unrepresentative, AI can create severe disparities in care[cite: 1]. A notable example mentioned is a US hospital algorithm that systematically underprivileged Black patients during resource allocation (Chinta et al. 2025, p. 2)[cite: 1].

### Justification on why I chose the artefact[cite: 1]
I selected this paper because AI in healthcare is a field I care deeply about, and it directly built on our workshop debate about whether algorithms belong in medical settings[cite: 1]. I located the paper via Google Scholar, filtering for recent publications from 2025[cite: 1]. While reading the abstract and conclusion gave me a high-level overview, I had to dig deeper into the main text to fully understand what "bias" meant in practice[cite: 1]. I was really taken aback to learn that diagnostic tools for depression often struggle with non-Western patients because they were trained almost exclusively on English-speaking, Western populations (Chinta et al. 2025, p. 2)[cite: 1]. Even though this study focused on the US healthcare system, it made me wonder how many similar, unchecked biases might be present in the technologies we import here into Australia[cite: 1].

### Summary of the artefact: My Personal Reflection[cite: 1]
One big revelation for me this week was learning that AI isn't a brand-new concept—it's been around for decades, long before the current wave of generative tools[cite: 1]. It was fun looking at historical AI-generated images in class and seeing how crude or buggy they used to be[cite: 1]. We had some great discussions during the session, and I was surprised to find out just how many of my peers are already paying for premium subscriptions like ChatGPT Plus while I'm still using the free version[cite: 1].

### Justification on why I chose the artefact[cite: 1]
I snapped a photo during this part of the tutorial because the story behind IBM Watson really caught my attention[cite: 1]. Watson beat human champions on *Jeopardy!* back in 2011—15 years ago—which feels like a completely different era compared to ChatGPT's release in late 2022[cite: 1]. 

Our group decided to test Microsoft Copilot to see who it thought would win a match between Watson and ChatGPT today[cite: 1]. I naturally assumed ChatGPT would win due to its massive web dataset, but Copilot pointed out that Watson was explicitly engineered for precise rule-bound facts like *Jeopardy!* questions[cite: 1]. When we asked Copilot to simulate a game between them, it got confused trying to format the game show answers properly and ended up crashing on us[cite: 1]! 

That hands-on trial taught me a crucial lesson: Watson is a specialized, purpose-built tool designed to deliver verified factual outputs within a specific domain[cite: 1]. ChatGPT, on the other hand, is a broad conversational model built for natural dialogue—it doesn't fact-check itself and is prone to hallucinating[cite: 1]. Recognizing the gap between specialized tools and general LLMs was my biggest takeaway from the week[cite: 1].
