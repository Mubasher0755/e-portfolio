## Artefact 1: Nepal Blocks 26 Social Media Platforms

<img width="803" height="767" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/11e976b3-be98-4a0a-b1b9-74e9a4fdd4a9" />


🔗 [Al Jazeera – Nepal lifts social media ban after 19 killed in protests](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/9/9/nepal-lifts-social-media-ban-after-19-killed-in-protests-report)

**Summary**

In September 2025, Nepal's government blocked 26 social media platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and YouTube, saying it wanted to tackle misinformation, fraud and hate speech (Al Jazeera 2025). Young people responded with mass "Gen Z" protests against the ban, corruption and economic hardship. Nineteen people were killed by security forces, and the government withdrew the ban days later.

**Why I chose this artefact**

I chose this artefact because it is the clearest recent example of direct government censorship. Although officials presented it as a response to misinformation, many young Nepalis saw it as an attempt to silence dissent and control public opinion (CQUniversity 2026). It also shows that censorship can backfire when platforms are essential to daily life. However, not every restriction aims to silence critics, as Australia's approach shows.

## Artefact 2: Video – Why Did Australia Ban Social Media for Kids Under 16?

[![Why did Australia ban social media for kids under 16?](https://img.youtube.com/vi/aD4c-eLcPsQ/hqdefault.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aD4c-eLcPsQ)

▶️ [Watch on YouTube – ABC News](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aD4c-eLcPsQ)

**Summary**

In this ABC News (2025) video, Sophie Beren, founder of The Conversationalist, discusses why Australia banned social media for under-16s. From 10 December 2025, platforms such as TikTok, Instagram and YouTube must take "reasonable steps" to prevent Australians under 16 from having accounts (eSafety Commissioner 2026). The law aims to protect young people from harmful content and design features that encourage excessive screen time.

**Why I chose this artefact**

I chose this video because the law affects Australia directly and, unlike Nepal's ban, aims to protect rather than silence. Even so, it limits young people's access to information and expression, and two teenagers have challenged it in the High Court for restricting freedom of political communication (CQUniversity 2026). Both Nepal and Australia restrict access, but the European Union has tested a different approach: regulating the platform itself.

## Artefact 3: European Commission Fines X €120 Million

<img width="1897" height="803" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/0e6b8048-6ac6-4dc0-87e3-e3bc61d84180" />


🔗 [European Commission – Commission fines X €120 million under the Digital Services Act](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-fines-x-eu120-million-under-digital-services-act)

**Summary**

On 5 December 2025, the European Commission fined X €120 million in the first non-compliance decision under the Digital Services Act (European Commission 2025). The Commission found that X's paid blue checkmark misleads users, its advertising repository lacks transparency, and it prevents researchers from accessing public data. The fine was set according to the nature, gravity and duration of these breaches.

**Why I chose this artefact**

I selected this artefact because it shows a government holding a platform accountable without removing anyone's speech. European users keep full access to X; the rules target deception and secrecy instead. This answers the workshop question of who should regulate content: a public regulator setting transparency rules, not judging opinions. Judging all three cases requires an ethical test, which Mill's Principle of Harm provides.

## Artefact 4: Workshop 9 – Mill's Principle of Harm

<img width="1432" height="1079" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/287d42ce-c65f-4b84-8348-b0a062b04cd3" />


![Workshop 9 attendance selfie](images/workshop9-selfie.jpg)

**Summary**

Workshop 9 introduced John Stuart Mill's view of censorship (CQUniversity 2026). Mill opposed censorship because anyone can be wrong, so silencing an opinion may silence the truth. However, his Principle of Harm allows government to limit freedom to prevent harm to others, while protecting individuals from themselves is not a sufficient reason.

**Why I chose this artefact**

I chose this idea because it answers the workshop question of when protecting society becomes restricting freedom. Nepal's ban fails Mill's test, since it silenced criticism instead of preventing harm. Australia's law is harder: it mainly protects young people from harm to themselves, which Mill rejects for adults, although children may need special protection. The EU decision passes most clearly because it targets deception. Online control is justified only when it prevents genuine harm to others.
